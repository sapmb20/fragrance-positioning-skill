# Fragrance House Positioning Analysis

This skill produces a two-axis positioning map of fragrance houses with a written analysis, and makes every factual claim in it traceable to a source the reader can check. It works for any set of houses: heritage luxury maisons, designer labels, niche and artisanal brands, indie perfumers, mass-market and celebrity lines, direct sellers, and dupe brands.

There are two common entry points:
- **New analysis**: the user names a market or a set of brands and wants the map and write-up.
- **Existing draft**: the user already has a write-up (often in Google Docs) and wants the claims that need citations found and cited. Skip to step 6, but read the draft first and use steps 2–5 to check that the analysis holds up.

## Workflow

### 1. Scope
Establish, from the request or by asking only what can't be inferred:
- the **competitor set** (4–8 houses works best; fewer gives a thin map, more makes it unreadable),
- the **focal firm**, if the analysis is for one house in particular,
- the **market** (global, one country, one channel such as duty-free or e-commerce),
- the **deliverable**: a new document, or annotations on an existing one, and any length or format rules from an assignment.

If the user gives no competitor set, propose one that spans at least three archetypes from `house-archetypes.md`. A map where every house is in the same corner shows nothing.

### 2. Choose the axes
Pick two dimensions from `axes-library.md` that vary independently across the chosen houses. The most common mistake is two axes that move together (price and prestige, for instance), which collapses the map onto a diagonal and hides the real differences. Before committing, place the houses mentally: if they all fall along one diagonal, change an axis.

Fragrance as a category leans toward luxury, even at mass prices. Say so in the write-up, and define the prestige axis as a house's position *relative to the other houses*, not as an absolute "luxury vs. necessity" judgment.

Present the axes in a small table: axis, dimension, spectrum (low end → high end).

### 3. Place each house
For each house, give:
- **Quadrant** (e.g. "Low volume / High luxury"),
- **Evidence**: 3–5 short bullets of observable facts (distribution model, price tier, heritage, retail experience, ownership, signature products). These are the facts that will need citations in step 6, so keep each one specific and checkable.
- **Strategic implications**: what the position buys the house, whether it is sustainable, and the main risk (overexposure for a luxury house, price pressure for a mass one).

Placement is a judgment. Base it on the evidence bullets rather than general impressions, and note any house whose position differs by region or product line.

### 4. Read the map
Write what the map shows, in this order:
1. **Competing strategies**: which houses win on scarcity and prestige, and which on reach and value.
2. **Outliers**: a house that sits where the pattern says it shouldn't (high volume *and* high prestige, for example). Explain what lets it do that. This is usually the most interesting finding.
3. **White space**: any empty quadrant, followed straight away by why it might be empty for structural reasons (low volume raises unit costs, which makes low prices hard to sustain, for example). An empty quadrant is a question to investigate, not a ready-made opportunity.
4. **The marketing-mix point**: volume alone doesn't decide prestige. Distribution, heritage, retail experience, presentation, price and communication all shape perception.

### 5. Advantages and limitations
List both, as separate short lists. Typical advantages: it shows how marketing-mix elements interact, separates niche from mass competitors, hints at less-occupied space, and tests whether a position is sustainable. Typical limitations: perception is subjective, data may be incomplete or out of date, positions differ by region and product line (especially private or exclusive lines), perceptions of luxury change over time, two dimensions oversimplify, and an empty quadrant is not proof of opportunity.

If the user is a student, keep their own reflections ("what I learned") in their own voice. Don't rewrite their analysis unless they ask.

### 6. Citations
Follow `references/citation-workflow.md`. In short:
- Mark every claim that states a fact about a house or the market (founding dates, distribution, ownership, sales rank, growth figures, trends). Analysis, opinion and the user's own reasoning don't need a source.
- Find a source for each claim, preferring primary and trade sources over aggregator blogs.
- **Open and read every source before citing it**, and record its exact title, author, date and URL. A citation the reader can't verify is worse than none, because it looks authoritative.
- If the best available source only partly supports a claim, cite it and tell the user what's missing, or suggest rewording the claim to match what the source says.
- If you can't identify a source (for example, an assigned video with no link), leave it out and ask the user for it. Never invent or guess a citation.
- Format everything in MLA 9: in-text citations at the claim, and a Works Cited page at the end.

### 7. Deliver
- **New document**: use the structure in `references/output-template.md`.
- **Existing Google Doc**: edit the same file in place, following `references/google-docs-editing.md`. Never create a copy or a second file. Read the document before editing, and re-read it afterward to confirm every citation landed.

Finish with a short summary for the user: what was added and where, which sources were used, and anything that still needs their input (a missing source, a claim that's only partly supported, a formatting choice such as font or spacing that their instructor may specify).
